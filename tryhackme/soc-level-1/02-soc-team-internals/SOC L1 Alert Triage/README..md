# SOC L1 Alert Triage

## Overview

This room introduces the concept and lifecycle of SOC alerts and explains how a SOC L1 analyst can systematically review, prioritise, and triage alerts.

The room covers the process from event generation to alert creation, alert properties, prioritisation, investigation, classification, and final resolution.

Alert triage is a core responsibility of SOC L1 analysts because alerts can represent potential cyberattacks that need to be identified and investigated.

## From Events to Alerts

An alert begins with an event such as:

* User login
* Process launch
* File download

The system records the event in its logs.

These logs can then be sent to security solutions such as:

* SIEM
* EDR

Organizations may generate millions of logs per day, while only a smaller number of events require investigation.

An alert is a notification generated by a security solution when a specific event or sequence of events occurs.

Alerts help SOC analysts focus their attention on suspicious or anomalous activity instead of manually reviewing millions of raw logs.

### Event-to-Alert Flow

```text id="6b9q3r"
EVENT
  ↓
SYSTEM LOG
  ↓
SIEM / EDR
  ↓
DETECTION LOGIC
  ↓
ALERT
  ↓
SOC ANALYST
```

## L1 Role in Alert Triage

SOC L1 analysts are the first line of defence and work with alerts extensively.

Their main responsibilities include:

* Reviewing alerts.
* Distinguishing between malicious and legitimate activity.
* Notifying L2 analysts when a real threat is identified.

Other SOC roles also participate in the alert triage process:

### SOC L2 Analysts

Receive alerts escalated by L1 analysts and perform deeper analysis and remediation.

### SOC Engineers

Ensure alerts contain enough information for efficient triage.

### SOC Manager

Tracks the speed and quality of alert triage to help ensure real attacks are not missed.

## Alert Properties

SOC alerts generally contain several important properties.

### 1. Alert Time

Shows when the alert was created.

The alert may trigger a few minutes after the actual event.

### 2. Alert Name

Provides a summary of what happened based on the detection rule.

Examples:

* Unusual Login Location
* Email Marked as Phishing
* Windows RDP Bruteforce
* Potential Data Exfiltration

### 3. Alert Severity

Defines the urgency of the alert.

Typical levels include:

* Low / Informational
* Medium / Moderate
* High / Severe
* Critical / Urgent

### 4. Alert Status

Shows whether an alert is currently being handled or whether triage has been completed.

Examples:

* New / Unassigned
* In Progress / Pending
* Closed / Resolved

### 5. Alert Verdict

Also known as alert classification.

It determines whether the alert represents a real threat or noise.

* True Positive — Real Threat
* False Positive — No Threat

### 6. Alert Assignee

Shows which analyst is responsible for reviewing the alert.

The assignee can also be referred to as the alert owner.

### 7. Alert Description

Explains what the alert is about.

It can include:

* Detection rule logic.
* Why the activity may indicate an attack.
* Instructions for triaging the alert.

### 8. Alert Fields

Provide the values and information that triggered the alert.

Examples include:

* Affected hostname
* Entered command line
* Other alert-specific information

## Alert Prioritisation

SOC analysts may have many alerts waiting for investigation, so they need to determine which alert should be handled first.

This process is called **Alert Prioritisation**.

The room presents a simple prioritisation approach:

### 1. Filter the Alerts

Only select alerts that are:

* New
* Unseen
* Unresolved
* Not already being investigated by another analyst

### 2. Sort by Severity

Prioritise:

```text id="4lqjpc"
Critical
   ↓
High
   ↓
Medium
   ↓
Low
```

Critical alerts are prioritised because they are expected to represent more significant threats and potentially greater impact.

### 3. Sort by Time

When alerts have the same priority, start with the oldest alerts before newer ones.

The reasoning is that an older potential breach may have already progressed further than a newer one.

## Alert Triage

Alert review by SOC analysts can also be called:

* Alert triage
* Alert handling
* Alert processing
* Alert investigation
* Alert analysis

This room uses the term **Alert Triage**.

The process can be divided into three main stages:

```text id="n0j5k8"
INITIAL ACTIONS
      ↓
INVESTIGATION
      ↓
FINAL ACTIONS
```

---

## Initial Actions

The initial steps are designed to ensure that the analyst takes ownership of the alert and does not interfere with alerts already being handled by another analyst.

The analyst should:

1. Assign the alert to themselves.
2. Move the alert to **In Progress**.
3. Review the alert details.
4. Familiarise themselves with the alert name, description, and key indicators.

---

## Investigation

Investigation is the most complex part of the triage process.

The analyst uses technical knowledge and SIEM or EDR logs to determine whether the activity is legitimate or suspicious.

The room provides several recommendations:

### 1. Understand Who Is Under Threat

Identify the affected:

* User
* Hostname
* Cloud environment
* Network
* Website

### 2. Understand the Action

Determine what activity the alert describes.

Examples:

* Suspicious login
* Malware
* Phishing

### 3. Review Surrounding Events

Look for suspicious activity occurring shortly before or after the alert.

### 4. Use Threat Intelligence

Use threat intelligence platforms or other available resources to verify the analysis.

---

## Workbooks

Some SOC teams create **Workbooks**, also known as playbooks or runbooks, to help analysts investigate specific categories of alerts.

These resources provide instructions that can support L1 analysts during the investigation process.

---

## Final Actions

After completing the investigation, the analyst must determine the alert verdict.

The two main classifications introduced in this room are:

```text id="zj5o9t"
              ALERT
                ↓
           INVESTIGATION
                ↓
        ┌───────┴───────┐
        ↓               ↓
 TRUE POSITIVE     FALSE POSITIVE
   Real Threat       No Threat
```

The analyst should then:

1. Determine the verdict.
2. Prepare a detailed comment explaining the analysis.
3. Explain the reasoning behind the verdict.
4. Return to the dashboard.
5. Move the alert to **Closed**.

Escalation and detailed alert commenting are covered further in the following rooms.

## Alert Triage Workflow

The complete process can be summarised as:

```text id="u1g6gk"
              ALERT
                ↓
        PRIORITISATION
                ↓
        INITIAL ACTIONS
                ↓
          INVESTIGATION
                ↓
            VERDICT
                ↓
        ┌───────┴───────┐
        ↓               ↓
 TRUE POSITIVE     FALSE POSITIVE
        │               │
        └───────┬───────┘
                ↓
          DOCUMENTATION
                ↓
             CLOSED
```

The exact workflow may vary slightly between SOC teams.

## Analyst Notes

### Key Takeaways

* Alerts help SOC analysts focus on suspicious activity instead of manually reviewing millions of raw logs.
* SOC L1 analysts are the first line of defence and handle alerts extensively.
* Understanding alert properties is essential before beginning an investigation.
* Alert prioritisation helps analysts determine which alerts should be investigated first.
* Severity and alert age are important factors in prioritisation.
* Alert triage includes initial actions, investigation, and final actions.
* During investigation, analysts should review the affected entity, activity, surrounding events, and available threat intelligence.
* Alerts are ultimately classified as True Positive or False Positive.
* The analyst must document the investigation and verdict before closing the alert.
